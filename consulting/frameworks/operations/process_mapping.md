# Process Mapping

## Category
Operations / Process Improvement

## Purpose
Make a business process visible by documenting its steps, inputs, outputs, decisions, handoffs, systems, and failure points.

## Core Question
How does the work actually flow from trigger to outcome, and where are the major sources of delay, cost, error, or unnecessary effort?

## Use When
- A workflow is slow, expensive, repetitive, or error-prone.
- The current process is poorly understood.
- Automation is being considered.
- Process bottlenecks or unnecessary handoffs are suspected.
- Different teams perform the same process differently.
- A current-state baseline is needed before redesign.

## Do NOT Use When
- The process is already clearly documented and understood.
- The problem is purely strategic and unrelated to operational workflow.
- The objective is market or corporate strategy.
- Mapping detail would not change the decision.

## Key Questions
- What triggers the process?
- What are the major process steps?
- Who performs each step?
- What systems are used?
- What information enters and leaves each step?
- Where are decisions made?
- Where are handoffs occurring?
- Where does work wait?
- Where are errors introduced?
- Which steps are repetitive?
- Which steps require human judgment?
- Which steps add value?
- Which steps do not add value?
- Where are the bottlenecks?
- What exceptions occur?
- What is the total cycle time?

## Required Inputs
- Process owner
- Process trigger
- Process steps
- Roles and teams
- Systems
- Inputs and outputs
- Handoffs
- Decision points
- Cycle time
- Waiting time
- Error information
- Exception cases
- Process volume
- Process frequency

## Analysis Process
1. Define the process boundary.
2. Identify the start trigger and desired end state.
3. Map the major process steps in sequence.
4. Document owners, systems, inputs, outputs, and decisions.
5. Identify handoffs and waiting points.
6. Measure time, volume, errors, and rework.
7. Identify bottlenecks and unnecessary work.
8. Separate normal flow from exceptions.
9. Identify redesign and automation opportunities.
10. Prioritize improvement opportunities by impact and feasibility.

## Expected Output
- Current-state process map.
- Process owners and handoffs.
- Bottlenecks and failure points.
- Rework and non-value-added activities.
- Candidate redesign opportunities.
- Candidate automation opportunities.

## Interpretation
The process map should reveal where work actually happens and where value is lost. Pay particular attention to waiting, handoffs, manual effort, exceptions, rework, and decision complexity.

## Limitations
- A map can reflect how a process is supposed to work rather than how it actually works.
- Complex processes may require multiple levels of detail.
- Process maps describe flow but do not automatically quantify business impact.
- Stakeholder bias can affect the map.

## Common Mistakes
- Mapping the ideal process instead of the actual process.
- Starting with solutions before documenting the current state.
- Ignoring exception paths.
- Ignoring waiting time.
- Treating every step as equally valuable.
- Failing to validate the map with people who perform the work.

## Decision Impact
High

## Consulting Judgment
Map the process at the level required to make the decision. The objective is not to create a beautiful diagram; it is to expose inefficiency, decision points, and automation opportunities.

## Framework Reasoning Rule
Understand the actual work before redesigning or automating it.

## Framework Chain
Business Problem
→ Process Mapping
→ Identify Bottlenecks / Manual Work
→ Root Cause
→ Redesign
→ Automation Feasibility
→ Implementation

## Framework Combination Rules
- Combine with AI Feasibility when AI automation is being evaluated.
- Combine with Human-in-the-Loop when judgment points remain.
- Combine with Bottleneck Analysis when cycle time or throughput is the problem.
- Combine with Pareto Analysis when many failure types exist.
- Combine with Value Stream Mapping when end-to-end flow efficiency is the primary objective.

## Framework Selection Hierarchy
1. Use early when the workflow is poorly understood.
2. Establish the current state before proposing automation.
3. Increase detail around decision points, bottlenecks, and high-cost steps.
4. Transition to specialized analysis once the key operational issue is visible.
